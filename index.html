How American Cars Lost Their
Color

In 2025, just six colors accounted for 96% of new cars sold in North America. Four of them are grayscale. And the two that aren't — blue and red — have quietly become the darkest, most restrained versions of themselves. This is the story of a market that stopped choosing color.

02 — The real finding

Even the color cars are hiding

Here's the part that's easy to miss. "Blue" and "red" sound like exceptions to the grayscale rule — but look at which blues and reds people actually buy. The popular blue is navy. The popular red is deep and muted. Industry paint suppliers report that the darkest blue shades are the ones gaining ground, chosen precisely because they don't shout.

navy · the popular blue

Blue that reads as black

Dark navy has gained traction since 2017 and is favored for premium perception and resale. It's picked for restraint — depth without attention.

The bright muscle-car reds of the past have given way to deeper, more subdued tones — the same move toward restraint, one shade over.

05 — How this was made

Methodology & assumptions

A portfolio piece is only as honest as its footnotes. Here's exactly what's solid, what's estimated, and where judgment calls were made. Tap any item to expand.

A1The 2025 numbers are exact and single-sourced
The current breakdown comes from Axalta's 73rd annual Global Automotive Color Popularity Report (Dec 2025), North America region: white 31, gray 21, black 19, blue 10, silver 8, red 7, green 2, yellow/gold 1, brown/beige 1. These are production/sales share figures, not registrations of cars already on the road.
A2"96%" comes from summing the top six categories
31 + 21 + 19 + 10 + 8 + 7 = 96. The remaining 4% is green (2), gold (1), and brown (1). Depending on the source and year this rounds to 96–97%, which is why the takeaway is framed as "roughly the entire market."
A3The pre-2000 history is directional, not precise
There is no single clean public dataset of U.S. car color going back to the 1970s. The decade figures and the trend line are stitched from secondary sources (PPG and Kelley Blue Book paint histories, iSeeCars, and registration analyses). Treat the shape of the curve as reliable and the exact decade percentages as estimates. The one firm anchor: white+black+gray were "just over 25%" of cars in the 1980s and "over 50%" by the 1990s.
A4"Even the color cars are dark" mixes data and observation
The claim that popular blues skew navy is supported by paint-supplier reporting (darker blue shades gaining share since 2017; navy preferred for premium/resale). The parallel claim about muted reds is more of a reasoned observation than a hard dataset — bright muscle-car reds have declined, but Axalta reports a single "red" category without shade-level splits. It's logged here as an interpretation, not a measured figure.
A5Swatch colors are representative, not official codes
The hex values used for each swatch (e.g. navy #1e3a5f, deep red #7c2d2d) were chosen to represent each category visually. They are not tied to any specific OEM paint code. The "low-light test" darkens each swatch by a fixed amount to illustrate perceptual convergence — it's a demonstration, not a photometric measurement.
A6Region and scope
All modern figures are North America unless noted. Global numbers differ slightly (white leads at ~29%, and the U.S. skews more toward white and trucks). "Cars" here includes light trucks and SUVs, which is how the industry reports it.
